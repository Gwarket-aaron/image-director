# Functional cinematography language

Use one primary choice from each relevant group. Do not add terminology only to make a prompt sound technical.

## Shot size

- Extreme wide: environment dominates; use for scale and spatial context.
- Wide: full subject and surrounding action are readable.
- Medium: subject action and body language share attention.
- Close-up: expression or one object detail becomes the main evidence.
- Extreme close-up: isolates a small feature; use only when context can be sacrificed.

## Camera angle

- Eye level: neutral relationship between viewer and subject.
- High angle: viewer looks down; useful for layout, vulnerability, or overview.
- Low angle: viewer looks up; useful for presence, scale, or structural emphasis.
- Top-down: spatial organisation is more important than depth.
- Dutch angle: introduces visible imbalance; use sparingly and intentionally.

## Composition

- Centred: stable and declarative.
- Rule of thirds: balanced subject and context.
- Symmetrical: controlled, formal, or architectural.
- Negative space: reserves visual quiet or room for text.
- Leading lines: guides attention through the frame.
- Layered depth: foreground, subject plane, and background remain distinct.

## Perspective feel

Describe the visual effect instead of inventing exact hardware:

- compressed distance
- natural perspective
- expanded foreground
- minimal distortion
- shallow spatial depth
- strong near-far separation

## Focus and depth

- Deep focus: multiple spatial layers remain readable.
- Shallow depth: isolate one focus target.
- Selective focus: specify exactly what is sharp.
- Soft background separation: retain context without competing detail.

## Motivated lighting

Name a plausible source and its visual consequence:

- soft window light from frame left
- hard afternoon sun creating long shadows
- overhead practical light with a controlled pool of illumination
- diffused studio light revealing product geometry
- screen light as a restrained secondary accent

## Verification rule

If a property cannot be observed or supplied, label it as an inference or omit it. A still frame alone does not verify focal length, camera body, movement, or creator intent.
